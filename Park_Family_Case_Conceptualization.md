# A Systemic Case Conceptualization of the Park Family

*BI Systems — Common Assignment*

---

## Introduction

The Park family presents for therapy at a developmental and cultural crossroads. Mr. and Mrs. Park are first-generation immigrants from Seoul, South Korea, raising two children—John (19), now a freshman at an Ivy League university, and Jackie (13), an eighth grader in an academic center—in the United States, far from their extended family of origin. The referral originated with Jackie's individual therapist, and Mrs. Park frames the presenting concern in relational terms: she "doesn't understand" her children, this lack of understanding distresses her, and she wants to grow closer to them. Both children carry histories of depressive symptoms, passive suicidal ideation, academic pressure, acculturative strain, and—in Jackie's case—an emerging gender and sexual identity not yet disclosed to the parents.

What follows applies a family-systems lens to the case across five domains: systems thinking, assessment, cultural variations, intervention, and ethical reasoning. Throughout, the guiding assumption is the foundational premise of general systems theory—that the family is a whole whose properties cannot be reduced to the sum of its individual members (von Bertalanffy, 1968).

---

## 1) Systems Thinking

### a. Boundaries

Boundaries are the rules that define who participates in a subsystem and how (Minuchin, 1974). In structural family therapy, boundaries fall along a continuum from rigid (disengaged) to diffuse (enmeshed), with a clear-but-permeable midpoint considered healthiest. General systems theory adds a complementary frame: every living system regulates the flow of information and energy across its boundary, and a family can be relatively *open* (exchanging readily with the surrounding environment) or *closed* (insulating itself) (von Bertalanffy, 1968). Bowen (1978) reframes the same phenomenon intrapsychically and intergenerationally through differentiation of self—the capacity to maintain a clear sense of self while staying emotionally connected.

The Park family illustrates several boundary patterns at once. The parental–child boundary around academics appears diffuse to the point of enmeshment: Mrs. Park spent the after-school hours teaching math and supervising instrument practice, and the parents "talk with [John] often" about whether he should drop out or leave the pre-med track—suggesting the parents are highly involved in a decision that, at 19 and away at college, properly belongs to John's individual subsystem. Simultaneously, the emotional boundary is rigid: Jackie fears Mrs. Park "won't understand and will ignore/minimize any emotions," and both children describe being unable to disclose their inner lives. The family thus shows the classic combination of enmeshment around achievement and disengagement around affect. At the system's outer boundary, the family has historically been relatively closed—therapy was a first-time experience, mental-health language was unfamiliar, and the secrecy demanded around the Chicago move ("told not to share this with anyone") modeled a low-permeability stance toward the outside world.

*Two ways this understanding shapes my approach:*

1. **I will work to recalibrate rather than eliminate boundaries.** Because enmeshment here is culturally embedded and well-intentioned (parental involvement as love), I will not pathologize closeness. Instead I will use structural techniques to open an emotional channel while age-appropriately firming the generational boundary—e.g., supporting John's autonomy over the pre-med decision while helping the parents express care that is not contingent on grades.
2. **I will treat the family's relative closure as an engagement variable, not resistance.** Knowing the system has been low-permeability, I will normalize therapy slowly, explicitly invite Mr. Park (whose English is more limited) into the room as a full participant, and frame the work as the family *opening a new channel of information*—language that aligns with how a closed system can safely become more open.

### b. Family Resiliency

Family resilience is the system's capacity to withstand and rebound from adversity, becoming strengthened through the process (Walsh, 2016). Walsh organizes resilience into three domains: belief systems (making meaning of adversity, maintaining a positive outlook, transcendence), organizational processes (flexibility, connectedness, social and economic resources), and communication processes (clarity, open emotional expression, collaborative problem-solving).

The Parks display substantial resilience alongside their vulnerabilities. Organizationally, the family is intact, economically stable, and highly connected; the parents have already mobilized resources by initiating therapy for both children—a significant adaptive move for a family new to mental-health care. In the belief domain, the parents place enormous value on education as a pathway to security, a meaning system that has produced real achievement. Their key resilience asset, however, is emerging: Mrs. Park has begun to question whether her academic focus "may be impacting his mood," which signals capacity for reappraisal—exactly the meaning-making flexibility Walsh identifies as central. The principal deficit lies in communication processes: emotional expression is constrained, and clear, collaborative dialogue about feelings is precisely what the family lacks.

*Two ways this understanding shapes my approach:*

1. **I will lead from strengths.** Rather than centering deficits, I will name and amplify what is already working—the parents' love, their willingness to seek help, the children's insight—because a resilience-oriented frame mobilizes the family's own competencies and reduces shame for parents who fear they have "done it wrong."
2. **I will target communication processes as the highest-leverage domain.** Walsh's model tells me the family's organizational and belief resources are relatively sound, so I will concentrate intervention on building clear, open, collaborative communication—the specific domain where change will most efficiently relieve the children's isolation and Mrs. Park's distress.

### c. The Expanded Family Life Cycle

The expanded family life cycle frames family development as a series of transitions, stressed by both vertical (multigenerational legacies, culture, immigration) and horizontal (predictable and unpredictable life events) stressors (McGoldrick et al., 2011). It deliberately decenters the "normal" middle-class life cycle to account for migration, culture, and social context as organizing forces.

The Parks are negotiating two simultaneous horizontal transitions: launching a young adult (John's departure for college) and parenting an adolescent (Jackie's individuation, identity exploration, and her own depressive history). Migration is the dominant vertical stressor: the move from Korea, and later the disruptive move to Chicago in John's eighth-grade year, function as off-time transitions that compounded ordinary developmental strain. McGoldrick et al. (2011) note that migration can add a developmental stage of its own, often felt acutely a generation later—which fits the children's struggle to integrate Korean and American identities and their distance from extended family who remain in Korea. Vertical legacies are also visible: Mrs. Park experienced her own mother's control over her studies and life decisions—a pattern she resents yet partly reproduces, illustrating multigenerational transmission.

*Two ways this understanding shapes my approach:*

1. **I will normalize the family's distress as transitional rather than dispositional.** Framing the current strain as the predictable turbulence of launching one child while individuating another—amplified by migration—reduces blame and gives the family a developmental map, which is itself therapeutic.
2. **I will explicitly bring the migration narrative and Mrs. Park's family-of-origin legacy into the room.** Because vertical stressors are driving so much of the horizontal difficulty, I will invite the parents to tell the immigration story and to reflect on the intergenerational pattern of academic control, opening space for the parents to choose what to carry forward and what to revise.

## 2) Assessment

In preparing for the first interview, my supervisor's two clinical questions—how the family defines the problem/solutions/resources, and what outside influences stress or support them—orient me to elicit the family's own framing before imposing mine.

### What I would expect the family to say

**Example 1 — Defining the problem and solutions/resources.** I would expect divergent problem definitions across the system. Mrs. Park is likely to define the problem as *her own lack of understanding*—"I don't know what is happening with my children and I want to help them"—and to locate the solution in learning (consistent with her belief that diligence and information solve problems). John and Jackie, by contrast, are likely to define the problem as *pressure and not being understood*—Jackie may say something like, "They only care about grades and they won't understand my feelings," and may not voluntarily name her identity exploration at all. The parents may identify few internal emotional resources but real external ones (the individual therapists, the schools, financial stability), while the children may name their friend groups and siblings as resources—relationships Mrs. Park, notably, partly distrusts ("unhealthy relationship dynamics").

**Example 2 — Outside influences that stress and support.** I would expect the family to describe family of origin in Korea as both a support (cultural identity, values, periodic contact) and a stressor (geographic distance, the absence of extended kin, and, for Mrs. Park, the inherited template of academic control). I would expect the highly competitive schools to be named as a major external stressor—John explicitly described a high school where "every kid was in competition with one another"—even as the parents may view those same schools as supports advancing the children's futures. The children would likely name the broader peer and cultural environment (struggling to connect with both same-age and Korean peers) as a stressor, and their mental-health-bonded friendships as a support.

### How those answers would guide treatment

**Guiding example 1 (problem/solution definition).** The mismatch—parents defining the problem as *understanding/knowledge* and children defining it as *pressure/emotional invisibility*—tells me the family lacks a shared problem definition, so my first treatment task is **building a common, relational frame** all members can endorse. I would reframe the presenting problem from "the children have problems" to "the family wants to understand one another and feel closer"—a definition that honors Mrs. Park's stated goal, validates the children's wish to be understood, and avoids scapegoating any member. This shared frame becomes the contract that holds the family in treatment.

**Guiding example 2 (outside influences).** Learning that the schools and the academic-achievement culture are powerful external stressors—while also being valued—tells me I cannot treat "academic pressure" as simply something to remove; it is woven into the family's identity and the surrounding ecology. This guides me toward an **ecological, both/and intervention strategy**: rather than asking the parents to abandon educational values, I will help the family renegotiate the *meaning and intensity* of those values, and I will consider engaging school-based supports (the social worker, counselors) as part of the treatment system, since the stressor lives partly outside the home.

## 3) Cultural Variations

### Three examples of culture interacting with the family system

1. **Collectivism, filial expectation, and the academic value system.** Korean and broader East Asian cultural frameworks emphasize family interdependence, parental authority, educational achievement, and the bringing of honor to the family (Kim & Park, 2009; Sue & Sue, 2019). In the Parks, this manifests as Mrs. Park's caregiving-and-tutoring role, the centrality of admission to elite universities, and the pressure on Jackie to match John's valedictorian achievement. What a Western individualistic lens might read as "over-involvement" is, in cultural context, an expression of parental devotion and a coherent value system. Culture and system are therefore inseparable here: the enmeshed academic boundary *is* the cultural value enacted structurally.

2. **Acculturation gap and acculturative family distancing.** The parents are Korean-dominant and first-language Korean; the children are English-dominant with limited Korean. Jackie explicitly states that part of why she cannot open up to her mother is that "sometimes she cannot find the words in Korean to express what she wants to say." This is a textbook instance of acculturative family distancing—the growing breach between immigrant parents and their more-acculturated children driven by communication breakdown and incongruent cultural values, a breach empirically linked to youth depression and family conflict (Hwang, 2006; Hwang & Wood, 2009). The language gap is not incidental; it is a structural feature of the family's communication subsystem.

3. **Cultural framing of mental health and help-seeking, intersecting with stigma.** This was the family's first experience with therapy, and Mrs. Park "did not grow up understanding mental health." East Asian cultural contexts often carry stigma around mental illness and a preference for somatic or academic framing of distress over emotional disclosure (Sue & Sue, 2019). This interacts with the system to suppress emotional communication—Jackie's fear that her mother will "minimize any emotions" reflects not maternal indifference but a cultural and generational unfamiliarity with the language of feelings. Layered onto this is Jackie's emerging asexual, aromantic, they/them identity, which intersects with cultural conservatism around gender and sexuality and intensifies the risk—and the stakes—of disclosure.

### Three things I would do to build rapport and work effectively

1. **Position myself as a learner and use a cultural-humility, both/and stance.** I would explicitly invite the parents to teach me about their values and the immigration story, validating education and family loyalty as strengths rather than problems to be corrected (Falicov, 1995; Sue & Sue, 2019). This counters the family's likely fear of being judged as "bad parents" by a Western therapist and respects the parental hierarchy that is culturally salient.

2. **Address language directly and equitably.** Because Mr. Park's English is limited and Mrs. Park currently translates for him, I would assess the need for, and offer, a qualified Korean-speaking interpreter or co-therapist so that Mr. Park participates as a full member rather than through his wife. I would also be attentive to the children's reduced Korean fluency, allowing the family to use whichever language carries the most emotional precision—naming the language gap itself as a workable clinical issue rather than an obstacle (Hwang, 2006).

3. **Honor hierarchy and pace disclosure.** I would engage the parents first and visibly, respecting their authority and the cultural expectation that elders are addressed with deference, before drawing out the children. I would also move carefully and protectively around Jackie's identity, never forcing or accidentally precipitating disclosure, and building trust with both generations before any conversation about identity occurs—recognizing the cultural and safety dimensions involved.

## 4) Intervention

For this case I draw on **Structural Family Therapy** (Minuchin, 1974), a theory whose central constructs—boundaries, subsystems, hierarchy, and enactment—map directly onto the Park family's presentation. (I have selected structural theory because it best fits the boundary patterns identified in Question 1; if your course foregrounded a different model, Bowenian therapy would be an equally strong fit given the multigenerational and immigration themes.)

### Intervention 1 — Joining and Accommodation

**Why it would help.** Before any restructuring is possible, the therapist must be admitted to the system. Joining—adapting to the family's style, language, and hierarchy—builds the alliance that makes later interventions tolerable (Minuchin, 1974; Minuchin & Fishman, 1981). For a culturally cautious, therapy-naïve family, joining is not preliminary niceness; it is the load-bearing intervention.

**When the therapist would know it is a good time.** Joining is front-loaded in the first sessions and revisited whenever tension spikes or a member withdraws—for instance, if Mr. Park grows quiet because the conversation has moved too fast for his English.

**What the therapist would say.** "Mr. and Mrs. Park, you've raised two accomplished children far from your own families, in a country that wasn't your first home—that takes enormous dedication. I'd like to understand how your family works and what matters most to you, so please correct me whenever I get something wrong." (Addressing the parents first respects the hierarchy; the invitation to correct signals collaboration.)

**How the therapist would know it helped.** Indicators include the family returning for sessions, increased spontaneous talk (especially from Mr. Park), parents seeking the therapist's view, and a felt easing of guardedness in the room.

### Intervention 2 — Enactment

**Why it would help.** Enactment asks family members to interact directly in session—rather than reporting to the therapist—so that boundary and communication patterns become observable and modifiable in real time (Minuchin & Fishman, 1981). Given the Parks' constricted emotional communication, an enactment makes the very problem (Jackie's fear of being minimized; the parents' difficulty hearing feeling) visible and workable.

**When the therapist would know it is a good time.** Once a basic alliance exists and a member describes a relational difficulty in the abstract—e.g., Jackie says she "can't talk to" her mother—that is the cue to shift from talking *about* to talking *with*.

**What the therapist would say.** "Jackie, instead of telling me, I'd like you to try telling your mom right now—even something small about how your week felt. Mrs. Park, your job is just to listen and then tell Jackie what you heard." The therapist then coaches and, if needed, blocks interruptions or minimizing responses to keep the channel open.

**How the therapist would know it helped.** Success looks like the family sustaining a direct emotional exchange, Mrs. Park reflecting feeling rather than redirecting to academics, and Jackie reporting she felt heard—small but real evidence that the rigid emotional boundary has become more permeable.

### Intervention 3 — Boundary Making (with reframing)

**Why it would help.** Boundary making strengthens or loosens the lines between subsystems—here, firming the generational boundary so John gains age-appropriate autonomy over his academic decisions, while loosening the rigid emotional boundary so feeling can flow (Minuchin, 1974). Pairing it with a reframe—recasting "pressure" as "love expressed through achievement"—lets the family change behavior without feeling their values are under attack, which is essential for a culturally invested family.

**When the therapist would know it is a good time.** This becomes appropriate once joining is secure and enactments have revealed the boundaries clearly—typically the middle phase of treatment—and especially when the parents express care in achievement terms ("we just want him to succeed").

**What the therapist would say.** "I hear how much you love John—and it sounds like grades have become the main language that love gets spoken in. What would it look like to also tell John he's enough, separate from any number? John, this decision about pre-med—what would help you feel it's truly yours, with your parents' support behind whatever you choose?"

**How the therapist would know it helped.** Helpfulness is evidenced by the parents voicing affection and acceptance not tied to performance, John reporting reduced pressure and greater ownership of his choices, and a measurable drop in the children's reported academic anxiety over subsequent sessions.

## 5) Ethical Reasoning

The following three ethical issues are distinct from the clinical content above and are drawn directly from the case material. I reason from the AAMFT *Code of Ethics* (2015) and the ACA *Code of Ethics* (2014).

### Issue 1 — Confidentiality, privacy, and Jackie's undisclosed identity

Jackie identifies as asexual, aromantic, and uses they/them pronouns, but uses she/her around the parents and has not disclosed this to them. In conjoint family therapy, "who is the client" and how secrets are handled is a core ethical challenge (AAMFT, 2015, Standard 2). I must avoid both betraying Jackie's confidence (which could cause harm, rupture trust, and—given cultural context—pose safety risks) and colluding in a secret that distorts the family work. **How I would handle it:** at the outset I would establish a clear, written no-secrets *or* limited-confidentiality policy and define how individually obtained information will be handled, so expectations are explicit before any disclosure. I would honor Jackie's pace and autonomy, never outing them; I would explore, in individual time or in age-appropriate ways, what Jackie wants, assess for safety, and—if and when Jackie chose to disclose—facilitate that conversation supportively. I would also seek consultation and document my reasoning, recognizing this as an area requiring competence in working with minors and LGBTQ+ clients (ACA, 2014, Standards B.5, A.4).

### Issue 2 — Informed consent and language access

Mr. Park's English is limited, and currently Mrs. Park translates therapy content for him. Using a family member—particularly one's spouse, who is also a client in the system—as interpreter compromises both the quality and the voluntariness of Mr. Park's informed consent and may filter or distort what he receives and expresses (ACA, 2014, Standard A.2.c; AAMFT, 2015, Standard 1.2). **How I would handle it:** I would arrange a qualified, neutral interpreter or, where possible, a Korean-speaking clinician, and provide consent materials in Korean, so that Mr. Park consents to and participates in treatment as a fully informed, equal member rather than through his wife. This protects his autonomy and the integrity of the therapeutic relationship.

### Issue 3 — Risk assessment, duty to protect, and managing multiple clients' welfare

Both children have histories of passive suicidal ideation and self-harm (John's cutting; both children's depressive presentations), and a family system under high pressure can re-escalate risk. The therapist has an ongoing ethical duty to assess and respond to risk and to protect client welfare, which can require breaching confidentiality if imminent danger emerges (ACA, 2014, Standard B.2; AAMFT, 2015, Standard 1.2). This intersects with the confidentiality concern above: if risk information arises—particularly tied to Jackie's identity stress—I may face a tension between Jackie's privacy and parental need-to-know. **How I would handle it:** I would conduct a thorough initial and ongoing risk assessment of both children, coordinate (with appropriate releases) with the existing individual therapists and schools to avoid fragmented or contradictory care, and establish clear safety planning. I would make explicit—at consent—the specific limits of confidentiality regarding danger to self, so that any necessary disclosure to parents occurs within a framework the family understood in advance, and I would document and consult throughout.

## Conclusion

Viewed systemically, the Parks are not a family of broken individuals but a loving system straining under the combined weight of migration, two simultaneous developmental transitions, an acculturation and language gap, and a value system that channels devotion almost entirely through achievement. Their resilience—intact bonds, mobilized resources, and Mrs. Park's dawning willingness to reconsider—provides genuine leverage. A culturally humble, structurally informed approach that joins the family on its own terms, opens the constricted emotional channel through enactment and boundary work, and proceeds with careful ethical attention to confidentiality, language access, and safety offers this family a credible path toward the closeness Mrs. Park named in the very first session.
